Wind in the Crosswinds

Why solar survives political risk and wind doesn't

Aims Data Charts Tools Conclusions

Aims

Climate policy is often discussed as if it affects "renewables" uniformly — a single category that either benefits or suffers from political change. This project tests whether that framing holds, by asking a narrower, more specific question: are wind and solar equally exposed to political risk, or does one absorb the shock while the other survives largely unscathed?

I examine two independent political events, one in each country, that changed the financial conditions for renewable energy almost overnight: the US "One Big Beautiful Bill Act" (2025), which rolled back federal tax incentives, and the UK's 2023 Contracts for Difference auction, where a mispriced cap produced zero bids for offshore wind — even as solar and onshore wind continued to secure contracts in that same auction. Comparing how solar and wind capacity responded to each shock lets me test whether a consistent pattern holds across two different countries and two entirely different political mechanisms (legislation vs. a single mispriced auction).

Beyond the comparison itself, I look more closely at one concrete case — a specific cancelled project — to show what this pattern means in practice, not just in aggregate statistics.

The answer matters for how climate policy gets designed: if wind and solar carry genuinely different exposure to political reversal, governments and investors need technology-specific safeguards, not a single blanket approach to "renewable energy policy."
